## Paper 1 — Jadli et al. (2022) ⭐ FULL READ
**Title:** Toward a Smart Lead Scoring System Using Machine Learning  
**Source:** Indian Journal of Computer Science and Engineering (IJCSE), Vol. 13, No. 2  
**URL:** https://www.ijcse.com/docs/INDJCSE22-13-02-098.pdf

### Summary
Compares multiple ML algorithms — Random Forest, Decision Tree, Logistic Regression, and others — for predicting lead conversion in B2C contexts. Uses a public leads dataset with behavioural and demographic features. Key findings: Random Forest achieves the highest accuracy (93.02%), followed by Decision Tree (91.47%). However, Decision Tree and Logistic Regression have significantly shorter training times, making them preferable in resource-constrained or latency-sensitive settings.

The paper frames traditional (rule-based) scoring as subjective and bias-prone, requiring expert knowledge for weight assignment. ML scoring is positioned as superior in data volume handling and automation. A clear table distinguishing traditional vs predictive scoring is provided.

### Key Findings
- RF: 93.02% accuracy — best performer
- DT: 91.47% accuracy, fastest training
- LR: lower accuracy but fastest inference — best for low-resource deployment
- Traditional rules are biased and rarely updated; ML detects patterns automatically

### Relevance to Our Paper
Directly comparable to our experimental setup. We use LR and RF against rule-based scoring. Their accuracy results provide a baseline expectation. **Cite in Related Work and Results Discussion.**

### Gap Identified
Does not compare rule-based vs ML head-to-head on same dataset. Does not address latency as a deployment metric. No analysis of class imbalance effects. Our paper addresses all three.

## Paper 2 — Nygård & Mezei (2020) ⭐ FULL READ
**Title:** Automating Lead Scoring with Machine Learning: An Experimental Study  
**Source:** HICSS-53 (Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences), January 2020  
**URL:** https://aisel.aisnet.org/hicss-53/da/machine_learning_in_finance/5/

### Summary
Studies how ML can automate lead scoring using historical behavioural data (browsing, email activity, contact data) to estimate purchase probability. Tests multiple aggregation strategies for time-series behavioural data to avoid model bias from activity windowing. Random Forest emerges as the best-performing model. Proposes that visual analytics can help extract business insights from model outputs.

Critically, this paper flags a methodological issue relevant to our work: how you window and aggregate behavioural activity data for converted leads affects model bias. We face the same issue in our synthetic data design.

### Key Findings
- RF outperforms other classifiers on behavioural lead data
- Data aggregation strategy significantly affects model bias
- Visual analytics improves interpretability of probabilistic scores

### Relevance to Our Paper
Motivates our use of behavioural features (emailOpens, websiteVisits, formFills) as scoring inputs. Supports our choice of RF as one of the two ML models. **Cite in Methodology and Related Work.**

### Gap Identified
Real CRM data with privacy constraints — cannot be reproduced. No comparison with rule-based systems. No latency or deployment resource analysis. Our synthetic dataset + rule-based comparison fills this gap directly.

## Paper 3 — Tiwari (2025)
**Title:** Advancing Client Risk Scoring: From Rule-Based Systems to Machine Learning Approaches  
**Source:** Journal of Computer Science and Technology Studies, Vol. 7, No. 8  
**URL:** https://al-kindipublishers.org/index.php/jcsts/article/view/10444  
**DOI:** 10.32996/jcsts.2025.7.8.1

### Summary
Examines the transition from rule-based to ML-based client risk scoring in banking/financial compliance. Argues that rigid rule systems fail against evolving fraud patterns and that compliance teams waste significant time on false positives generated by rule-based systems. Proposes ML as the solution, weaving together transaction history, behavioural analytics, and external data into risk profiles.

While the domain is financial risk rather than sales CRM, the core argument — that rule-based systems are brittle and ML systems are more adaptive — maps directly to our research question.

### Key Findings
- Rule-based systems produce high false positive rates in dynamic environments
- ML reduces unnecessary investigations by learning pattern shifts
- Human judgment remains necessary alongside ML — hybrid approaches recommended

### Relevance to Our Paper
Provides domain-transfer support for the rule-based vs ML argument in scoring systems broadly. Supports our H1 hypothesis that ML outperforms rules on F1. **Cite in Introduction and Related Work.**

### Gap Identified
Focuses on financial fraud, not sales lead conversion. No experimental comparison. No latency or resource analysis. Qualitative argument only — our paper provides quantitative evidence in the CRM context.

## Paper 5 — Japkowicz & Stephen (2002)
**Title:** The Class Imbalance Problem: A Systematic Study  
**Source:** Intelligent Data Analysis Journal, Vol. 6, pp. 429–449  
**URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/IDA-2002-6504

### Summary
A foundational study establishing the relationship between class imbalance, concept complexity, and classifier performance. Tests Decision Trees, Neural Networks, and SVMs under varying imbalance ratios. Finds that class imbalance systematically degrades classifier performance, especially for minority class recall, and that re-sampling and cost-sensitive methods are the most effective mitigations.

### Key Findings
- Class imbalance significantly harms Decision Tree and SVM performance
- Neural Networks are somewhat more robust to imbalance
- Re-sampling (oversampling minority, undersampling majority) is effective mitigation
- AUC-ROC is a more reliable metric than accuracy for imbalanced datasets

### Relevance to Our Paper
Our dataset has ~35–40% conversion rate (minority class). This paper justifies our use of F1 and AUC-ROC as primary evaluation metrics rather than accuracy. Also motivates reporting class balance percentages in the Dataset section. **Cite in Methodology (metrics justification) and Dataset section.**

### Gap Identified
Predates modern CRM ML applications by 20+ years. No domain-specific CRM findings. Does not address rule-based vs ML comparison. Foundational theory only.

## Research Gap Statement (Draft — 200 words)

Existing literature demonstrates that ML models, particularly Random Forest and Logistic Regression, outperform traditional rule-based approaches in lead scoring accuracy (Jadli et al., 2022; Nygård & Mezei, 2020). Enterprise CRM platforms such as Salesforce Einstein have successfully deployed AI-driven scoring at scale. However, a critical gap remains: **no study directly compares rule-based and lightweight ML lead scoring systems within a single, reproducible low-resource CRM environment**, measuring both predictive accuracy and operational latency simultaneously.

Most existing comparisons either use proprietary datasets that cannot be reproduced, evaluate ML models in isolation without a rule-based baseline, or operate at enterprise scale where resource constraints are irrelevant. SME and startup CRM deployments — which represent the majority of CRM deployments globally — face real constraints: limited training data, no dedicated ML infrastructure, and latency requirements incompatible with heavy models.

This paper addresses this gap by implementing both a deterministic rule-based scoring system and two lightweight ML classifiers (Logistic Regression and Random Forest) within the same NestJS/FastAPI CRM stack, evaluated on a controlled synthetic dataset of 1,000 leads with three noise levels (σ=5, 10, 15). We measure F1, AUC-ROC, and inference latency, providing the first direct head-to-head comparison in a low-resource CRM context.
